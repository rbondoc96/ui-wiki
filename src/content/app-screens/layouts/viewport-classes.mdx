## Five classes

Android's <Term id="window-size-class">window size classes</Term> give a
ready-made set of breakpoints that line up with real devices. They're defined
in dp, but dp and CSS pixels are both density-independent units, so the
numbers carry over to the web as-is.

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "android-window-size-classes", note: "Width classes and their ranges: compact under 600dp, medium 600 to 839, expanded 840 to 1199, large 1200 to 1599, extra-large 1600 and up.", role: "defines" }]}>

| Class       | Width (CSS px) | Typical screens                  | Layout                      |
| ----------- | -------------- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Compact     | under 600      | Phones                           | Tab bar, one pane           |
| Medium      | 600 to 839     | Tablets in portrait, foldables   | Rail, one pane              |
| Expanded    | 840 to 1199    | Tablets in landscape, laptops    | Rail or sidebar, two panes  |
| Large       | 1200 to 1599   | Laptops, desktop monitors        | Sidebar, two panes          |
| Extra-large | 1600 and up    | Large and ultrawide monitors     | Sidebar, two or three panes |

</SourceTrail>

Tailwind's default breakpoints (640, 768, 1024, 1280, 1536) don't line up with
these. Either is fine; what matters is one named set used everywhere. To use
the classes directly, add them to the theme:

```css
@theme {
  --breakpoint-medium: 37.5rem; /* 600px */
  --breakpoint-expanded: 52.5rem; /* 840px */
  --breakpoint-large: 75rem; /* 1200px */
  --breakpoint-xlarge: 100rem; /* 1600px */
}
```

That makes `expanded:grid-cols-2` and friends available alongside the defaults.

## Phones

One pane at a time, a bottom tab bar, and the list pushing to the detail as in
<a href="/docs/app-screens/layouts/master-detail">master-detail</a>.

- **Thumb reach.** Primary nav goes at the bottom, where one-handed use can
  reach it. Destructive or rare actions can live at the top.
- **Safe areas.** Notches and home indicators cover the edges. Add
  `viewport-fit=cover` to the viewport meta tag and pad fixed bars with
  `env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`, or the tab bar sits under the home indicator.
- **Landscape is short.** A phone on its side is wide but only about 400px
  tall, and a fixed top bar plus a tab bar can eat a third of that. Hide or
  shrink fixed bars when the height is small, not just when the width is.

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "wcag-target-size-minimum", note: "SC 2.5.8 (AA): pointer targets at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with spacing exceptions.", role: "supports" }]}>

Touch targets need room. WCAG 2.5.8 sets a floor of **24 by 24 CSS pixels**;
the platform guidelines ask for more (44pt on iOS, 48dp on Android), and those
are the sizes that feel right under a thumb.

</SourceTrail>

## Tablets

The awkward middle. A tablet in portrait is about as wide as a small laptop
window, but it's driven by touch.

- **Rail, not tabs.** At 600px and up, a bottom tab bar stretches into a wide
  strip of mostly empty space. A vertical rail on the left holds the same
  destinations.
- **Still one pane in portrait.** Two panes at 700px leave both cramped.
  Landscape usually crosses into expanded, where the split earns its place.
- **No hover.** Row actions that appear on hover never appear on a tablet. Gate
  hover-only affordances on the input, not the width:

```css
/* Hide row actions behind hover only where hover exists. */
@media (hover: hover) {
  .row-actions { opacity: 0; }
  .row:hover .row-actions,
  .row:focus-within .row-actions { opacity: 1; }
}
```

## Desktop and wide

This is the range most apps are designed at, and the
<a href="/docs/app-screens/layouts/nav-rail-triptych">nav-rail + triptych</a>
fits it naturally: nav, list, and content side by side. Between expanded and
large the main change is the nav itself: a rail of icons at the narrow end, a
sidebar with full labels once there's room. Let users collapse the sidebar back
to a rail, and remember their choice.

## Ultrawide

Past 1600px, and especially on 21:9 monitors at 2560 to 3440px, the failure
mode reverses. Nothing needs to be dropped; the risk is **stretching**.

- **Cap the reading width.** A paragraph that runs 300 characters wide is hard
  to read: the eye loses its place returning to the next line. Keep text blocks
  near 60 to 80 characters with a `max-width` in `ch`.
- **Spend the width on panes.** An inspector, a preview, a second document side
  by side. Extra panes use the room; a wider paragraph wastes it.
- **Keep content near the nav.** Centering a capped column on a 3440px screen
  can leave 25cm or more of empty screen between the sidebar and the content.
  Anchor the content next to the nav and put extra panes on the far side, or
  cap and center the whole shell.
- **Data is the exception.** Tables, boards, and dashboards can use the width
  directly: more columns, more cards per row. The cap is for prose, not grids.

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "wcag-visual-presentation", note: "SC 1.4.8 (AAA): blocks of text no wider than 80 characters, or 40 for CJK.", role: "supports" }]}>

<Callout variant="info" title="Where the 80 comes from">
  WCAG 1.4.8, a Level AAA criterion, asks that blocks of text be no wider than
  80 characters (40 for Chinese, Japanese, and Korean). `max-width: 80ch` is a
  direct way to meet it.
</Callout>

</SourceTrail>

## Width isn't the whole story

- **Input.** A touchscreen laptop is wide and touch-driven; an iPad with a
  trackpad is narrow-ish and precise. Use `(pointer: coarse)` and
  `(hover: hover)` for input-dependent behavior, and width for layout.
- **Height.** Landscape phones and 768px-tall laptops run out of vertical room
  first. Fixed headers, footers, and tall toolbars are the usual culprits.
- **Container size.** A pane inside a split is narrower than the window. When a
  component should respond to its own space, use container queries (see
  <a href="/docs/app-screens/layouts/responsive-app-shell">responsive
  app-shell</a>).
- **Zoom.** Browser zoom shrinks the CSS width. A 1280px window at 200% zoom is
  a 640px layout, so desktop users who zoom land in the tablet class.

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "wcag-reflow", note: "SC 1.4.10 (AA): content reflows at 320 CSS px without two-dimensional scrolling, equivalent to 1280px at 400% zoom.", role: "supports" }]}>

WCAG 1.4.10 (AA) asks that content work at **320 CSS pixels** wide without
scrolling in two directions, the equivalent of a 1280px window at 400% zoom.
That makes 320 the narrowest width to test, below any phone in common use.

</SourceTrail>

## Widths to test

| Width        | Stands in for                                      |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| 320          | Reflow floor, and a zoomed desktop                 |
| 390          | A current phone in portrait                        |
| 768          | A tablet in portrait                               |
| 1024         | A tablet in landscape, a small laptop              |
| 1440         | A common laptop and desktop width                  |
| 2560 to 3440 | Large and ultrawide monitors                       |

Check each with touch emulation on at the narrow end, and check a phone in
landscape for height.

<Callout variant="tip" title="This page, in one line">
  Name five width classes and use them everywhere; shed regions toward the
  phone, switch tabs to a rail at tablet width, and on ultrawide screens cap
  text near 80 characters and spend the extra room on panes.
</Callout>
