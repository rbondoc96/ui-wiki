Every other page in this section is **product** UI, where the design serves the
work. A landing page is the opposite: the design _is_ the product. Its job isn't
to let someone do a task, it's to make a single argument and offer a single
action, top to bottom, in scroll order. That flips most of the rules the rest of
the wiki runs on.

<LandingAnatomy />

## The zones, in scroll order

A conventional landing page stacks the same beats, each doing one job:

1. **Top bar** — logo, a few links, one primary action. Minimal, often sticky;
   it's a wayfinder, not a full nav.
2. **Hero** — one clear claim, a supporting line, and the primary CTA. It has to
   earn the first screen: say what this is and why it matters before any scroll.
3. **Social proof** — customer logos, a number, a quote. Borrowed credibility,
   placed early because trust is the first objection.
4. **Value props** — a few benefits framed as **outcomes**, not a feature dump.
   Each answers "what do I get," not "what does it have."
5. **Closing CTA** — repeat the one action now that the case is made. Don't make
   a convinced reader scroll back up to find it.
6. **Footer** — secondary nav, legal, the links scanners exit through.

## Different rules from app UI

What's correct on a dashboard is often wrong here.

- **Density inverts.** App UI rewards fitting more in view; a landing page rewards
  space, rhythm, and one idea per screen. Generous whitespace is doing work, not
  wasting it.
- **One primary action.** A dashboard exposes many controls; a landing page
  pushes **one** CTA, repeated. Competing calls to action split attention and
  convert worse than a single clear one.
- **It's a narrative, not a tool.** The layout is a sequence with a beginning and
  an end, read once, not a surface returned to daily. Order is the argument.
- **Visual ambition is on-brief.** Restraint is the right instinct for product UI;
  a landing page is allowed, often expected, to be bold. This is the one place in
  the wiki where "make it striking" is the correct note.

<DoDont>
  <Do title="One argument, one action, in order">
    Lead with a specific claim, support it with proof and outcomes, and ask for a
    single action, repeated. Each section should move a skeptical reader one step
    toward the CTA.
  </Do>
  <Dont title="A generic hero over identical feature cards">
    A vague headline, a gradient hero, three look-alike feature cards, and five
    competing buttons is the default that converts no one. It looks like a landing
    page without making an argument or asking for a decision.
  </Dont>
</DoDont>

<Callout variant="warning" title="The slop landing">
  The most common AI-generated marketing page is a big gradient headline, a row
  of three identical icon-and-text cards, and a wall of equally-weighted
  sections. It reads as generated because it makes no specific claim and shows no
  hierarchy. Say something only you could say, give one section clear primacy,
  and cut the cards that exist only to fill a three-column grid.
</Callout>

## It's still a page

Brand ambition doesn't excuse the fundamentals. The hero is often the largest
asset on the site, so its loading cost is the first-impression cost: size images,
avoid layout shift as fonts and media arrive (the same
<a href="/docs/app-screens/rendering">rendering</a> concerns, now with money on
the line). It must be reachable by keyboard and screen reader, and it has to hold
up from a 390px phone to a wide desktop. A striking page that paints slowly or
breaks on a phone loses the conversion it was built to win.

This is the brand-register counterpart to the product-register
<a href="/docs/app-screens/layouts/dashboard-layout">dashboard</a>: same wiki, two
opposite jobs, two opposite sets of rules.

<Callout variant="tip" title="This archetype, in one line">
  A landing page is a scroll-ordered argument for one claim ending in one action;
  it inverts app-UI density, rewards visual ambition, and still has to load fast
  and work everywhere.
</Callout>
