The shell behind Linear, Superhuman, and nearly every modern mail client is the
same four-region frame: a thin **nav rail** of contexts, a filterable **list**,
a **content** panel for the selected item, and a **metadata** rail of facts and
cross-references. It is a master-detail layout with a navigation rail bolted to
the front and a context rail bolted to the back, and it earns its popularity by
keeping _navigate_, _scan_, _read_, and _relate_ visible at the same time so the
user never loses their place.

<Callout variant="info" title="Why “triptych”?">
  A triptych is an artwork in three hinged panels: a wide central scene flanked
  by two narrower wings. The name carries over because the heart of this layout
  is exactly that, the **list**, **content**, and **metadata** panels standing
  side by side, with the content panel widest in the middle. The nav rail isn't
  one of the three; it's the frame the triptych hangs on, which is why the
  pattern is named **nav-rail + triptych** rather than a four-panel anything.
</Callout>

## Anatomy

Each region answers a different question, and the discipline of the pattern is
that none of them tries to do a second job.

### Rail — where am I?

A thin column of top-level contexts (Inbox, Pull Requests, Jira, Notes…) with
settings or account pinned to the bottom. It is chrome, not content: it changes
_what_ the list shows, never the selection inside it. Keep it to icons, expand
to labels on hover or toggle, and collapse it first when width gets tight. It's a
sidebar in the sense of the <a href="/docs/app-screens/navigation/nav-pattern-picker">nav
pattern picker</a>, chosen here because the contexts are many and may nest.

### List — what's here, and which one?

The per-context column, topped by a filter and toolbar header (state, "mine",
needs-review, repo, search). Its only job is to make one item findable and
selectable. Rows are dense and scannable: a status marker, a title, a line of
secondary text. Selecting a row drives everything to its right.

### Content — the thing itself

The widest region, where the selected item is rendered in full: a PR diff, an
email thread, a rendered note. A **Linked** block here is what turns a list of
records into a graph, surfacing the issues, notes, and PRs tied to this one.

### Metadata — what relates to it?

A narrow rail of facts about the selection (author, dates, reviewers) and
cross-references out to other contexts. It is the first region to fold away when
space is short, because it supports the content rather than carrying it.

Drive the live shell below: switch contexts in the rail, select a row to swap
both right-hand panels, and collapse the rail or the metadata column to watch
the triptych fold to two panes.

## When it wins

This layout is the right call when the work is **browse-then-act on a stream of
similar items**: triaging a queue, working an inbox, reviewing PRs, moving
tickets. It pays off most when:

- Items share a shape, so one list and one content renderer cover them all.
- Users keep their place while ranging across many items (the list stays put).
- The work is **keyboard-first** and high-volume; the fixed frame lets `j`/`k`
  and shortcuts move through items without re-orienting on every navigation.
- Items relate to each other, so the metadata rail and a Linked block earn their
  keep.

## When it breaks

The frame turns into a cage when the content stops fitting the mold.

- **Wide content.** Tables, canvases, dashboards, and diff views fight a list
  and a metadata rail for horizontal room. Below ~1100px you are choosing which
  region to starve.
- **One-off screens.** Settings, billing, or an onboarding flow have no "list of
  peers" to scan; forcing them into the middle pane leaves two empty rails.
- **Shallow apps.** Three destinations and no per-context list means the rail and
  list are pure overhead. Reach for top nav instead.
- **Mobile.** Four columns cannot coexist on a phone; the layout _must_ degrade
  to a single-pane drill-down (see below).

<DoDont>
  <Do title="Let the rail collapse, the metadata hide">
    Make the rail collapse to icons and the metadata rail hideable, content-out.
    The list and content are the load-bearing panes; the other two are
    affordances that yield space on demand.
  </Do>
  <Dont title="Pin all four panes at every width">
    Four fixed columns at 1280px become four cramped columns at 900px and an
    unusable mess on a tablet. A frame that can't shed regions can't follow the
    user off the desktop.
  </Dont>
</DoDont>

<Callout variant="warning" title="Don't make the list a second nav">
  The rail switches contexts; the list selects within one. When list rows start
  navigating to unrelated screens, you've grown a second navigation system, and
  users lose the one guarantee this layout makes: that selecting a row only ever
  changes the content to its right.
</Callout>

## Collapsing down: from triptych to one pane

The four regions are a priority order, not a fixed grid. As width shrinks, shed
from the outside in: metadata folds to a drawer or a tab, the rail collapses to
icons, and finally the list and content split into separate screens. The web and
native stacks reach the same single-pane endpoint by different routes.

<PlatformTabs>
  <Platform label="Web">
    Drive it with breakpoints and container queries. Wide: all four panes.
    Medium: metadata becomes a toggle drawer over the content, rail collapses to
    icons. Narrow: it's a master-detail pair where the list and content are two
    routes (`/prs` and `/prs/128`), so the URL still deep-links a single item and
    the back button returns to the list. The rail becomes a bottom tab bar or a
    hamburger.
  </Platform>
  <Platform label="React Native">
    There's no fluid resize; you branch on device class. Tablet landscape can run
    the list + content split (React Navigation's two-column layouts, Expo
    Router's parallel segments). Phone collapses to a native stack: the list is
    one screen, tapping a row pushes the content screen, the metadata becomes a
    section inside it or a bottom sheet. The rail is a bottom tab bar, not a side
    column. Same priority order, expressed as a push navigator instead of a
    grid.
  </Platform>
</PlatformTabs>

## Variants

The same skeleton stretches across a few common shapes:

- **Two-pane split (no metadata).** Drop the metadata rail and fold its facts
  into the content header. The Linear/GitHub-issue default; the simplest version
  of the pattern.
- **Metadata as a drawer.** Keep the facts, but slide them in over the content
  on demand instead of holding a permanent column. Buys content width at the
  cost of a click.
- **Expanding rail.** The rail toggles between an icon strip and a labelled
  column, sometimes absorbing the list entirely (the "collapse to icons" Linear
  move) for a focused, two-pane read.
- **Three-pane mail.** Folders rail, message list, message body, with the
  metadata folded into the message header. The oldest member of this family and
  the reason the shape feels so familiar.

  A navigation rail, a scannable list, the selected thing, and what relates to
  it, with the outer two regions designed to fold away the moment space runs
  short.
</Callout>
