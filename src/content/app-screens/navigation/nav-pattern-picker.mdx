"Where does navigation live" is a decision you make on every new app, and the
three answers are nearly always the same: a **bottom tab bar**, a **sidebar**, or
a **top nav**. The right one falls out of three properties of your app, not of
your taste: how many top-level destinations there are, how deeply they nest, and
what the platform expects. If those counts aren't settled yet, start with
<a href="/docs/app-screens/navigation/product-shape">choosing nav from product
and user flows</a>.

## The three chromes

### Bottom tabs

A fixed row of three to five destinations at the bottom of the screen, each flat
and equal. Wins on mobile, where the bottom edge is the reachable zone and the
small count keeps every destination one tap away. The hard ceiling is real: past
five tabs they shrink to unusable, and a "More" tab is a confession that the
model doesn't fit.

### Sidebar

A vertical list down the left edge, the only chrome that holds a **hierarchy**:
sections, groups, nesting, collapsible trees. Wins on desktop dashboards and
tools with many destinations. It trades horizontal room for that capacity, so it
collapses to icons or slides away when content needs the width. This is the rail
in the
<a href="/docs/app-screens/layouts/nav-rail-triptych">nav-rail + triptych</a>.

### Top nav

A horizontal bar across the top, usually with a logo and a handful of links.
Wins for **shallow** apps and marketing or content sites: few destinations, no
depth, a familiar web convention. It doesn't scale to many items (they overflow
or hide behind a hamburger) and can't express hierarchy on its own.

## Deciding axes

Read your app against three properties and the answer is usually forced.

| If…                                  | Lean toward    | Because                                          |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| 3–5 flat destinations, mobile-first  | **Bottom tabs**| Thumb-reachable, every destination one tap away  |
| Many destinations, or any nesting    | **Sidebar**    | The only chrome that holds hierarchy             |
| Few destinations, shallow, web/content | **Top nav**  | Familiar, light, no depth to express             |
| Deep tool that's also used on phones | **Sidebar + tabs** | Sidebar on desktop, a tab subset on mobile  |
| Few today, many within a year        | **Sidebar**    | Switching chrome later moves everything users memorized |

<DoDont>
  <Do title="Match the count to the chrome">
    Keep bottom tabs to five flat destinations. If the model is deeper, switch to
    a sidebar that can group and nest, rather than forcing depth into a bar that
    can't show it.
  </Do>
  <Dont title="Overload the chrome you picked">
    Eleven tabs hiding a tree behind a "More" sheet, or a top nav with three
    levels of dropdowns. When the navigation needs more than the chrome can show,
    the chrome is wrong, not too small.
  </Dont>
</DoDont>

## Combinations

Real apps mix these by scale rather than choosing one forever.

- **Sidebar + top bar.** Sidebar for primary destinations, a top bar for search,
  account, and global actions. The default for dense dashboards.
- **Responsive swap.** The same app shows a sidebar on desktop and a bottom tab
  bar (a subset of the same destinations) on mobile. Keep the destination set
  consistent so muscle memory survives the resize.
- **Tabs + stack.** Bottom tabs for top-level sections, a push/stack navigator
  inside each tab for depth. The standard mobile shape.

## Bottom bar or hamburger menu

On a phone, top-level navigation usually lands in one of two places: a bar of
visible tabs along the bottom, or a hamburger menu, the ☰ button that opens a
list. The trade is **visibility against capacity**. The ☰ icon itself is widely
recognized now; the cost is that everything behind it stays out of sight.

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "nng-hamburger-menus", note: "179 participants on 6 live sites: hidden navigation cut content discoverability by more than 20%; tasks were 15% slower on mobile (vs. combo) and at least 39% slower on desktop. Recommends visible links on mobile with 4 or fewer top-level items.", role: "supports" }]}>

**Hidden navigation gets used less.** In a 2016 Nielsen Norman Group study of
179 people on six live sites, hiding the navigation behind a menu cut how often
people found content by more than 20%. Tasks took 15% longer on mobile and at
least 39% longer on desktop. Their advice for phones: with four or fewer
top-level links, show them; with more, hide some, and back the menu up with
links in the page itself.

</SourceTrail>

| Lean toward       | When                                                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Bottom bar**    | Three to five destinations that people switch between often, in an app or app-like web app |
| **☰ menu**        | More than five destinations that can't be cut, or short visits that arrive from search and links |
| **Both**          | A few destinations used constantly, plus a long tail used rarely         |

Content and marketing sites lean toward ☰ because visitors read a page or two
and leave; moving between sections is the exception. Daily-use apps lean
toward the bar because switching sections is the whole job.

### Mixing the two

- **Tabs plus a Menu tab.** Four frequent destinations as tabs, and a fifth
  "Menu" tab for account, settings, and help. That's different from the "More"
  tab warned about above: a Menu tab holds the secondary items, while a More
  tab hides primary destinations that didn't fit.
- **A few links next to the ☰.** A website keeps its one or two most important
  links visible, "Pricing" and "Sign in" say, and puts the rest in the menu.
- **Show what fits.** A top strip shows as many destinations as the width
  allows and moves the rest into a "More" menu, so a wider phone or a tablet
  shows more without a separate design.

### Platform notes

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "android-layout-nav-patterns", note: "The navigation bar holds three to five destinations; a drawer can hold more but isn't as ideal because users must reach for the top bar on compact sizes. The bar becomes a navigation rail on large screens.", role: "supports" }]}>

**Android** recommends a navigation bar for three to five destinations. A
navigation drawer can hold more, but Android's own guidance calls it less ideal
on phones because opening it means reaching for the top of the screen. On
larger screens the bar becomes a rail. **iOS** apps use a tab bar for top-level
sections.

</SourceTrail>

<SourceTrail refs={[{ id: "macworld-safari-single-tab", note: "iOS 15 Safari defaults to a bottom Tab Bar layout; Settings > Safari > Tabs offers Single Tab to move the address bar back to the top.", role: "supports" }]}>

**Mobile websites** have a problem native apps don't. Since iOS 15, Safari on
iPhone puts its address bar and toolbar at the bottom by default (people can
switch back to the top in Settings). A fixed bottom bar on a website sits
directly above the browser's own, and shifts as the browser's toolbar shrinks
and grows on scroll. If a website ships one anyway, pad it with
`env(safe-area-inset-bottom)`, size full-height layouts with `dvh` instead of
`vh` so they follow the toolbar, and test in Safari with its default layout.

</SourceTrail>

## Web vs. native expectations

The platform sets the default, and fighting it costs familiarity.

<PlatformTabs>
  <Platform label="Web">
    Top nav and sidebars are the native idioms; users expect global links up top
    and a left sidebar in dashboards. Each destination should be a real URL so it
    deep-links and the back button works. A bottom tab bar on the desktop web
    reads as a mobile port.
  </Platform>
  <Platform label="React Native">
    The bottom tab bar is the platform-native primary nav on phones (React
    Navigation's tab navigator, Expo Router tab groups); a left sidebar feels
    foreign on a handset. Tablets can promote to a sidebar or split view. Tabs
    typically each own a stack navigator, so depth lives inside a tab rather than
    in the bar.
  </Platform>
</PlatformTabs>

<Callout variant="tip" title="This decision, in one line">
  Count the top-level destinations and check whether they nest: 3–5 and flat
  wants tabs, many or nested wants a sidebar, few and shallow wants a top nav, and
  the platform breaks any tie. On a phone, keep frequent destinations visible in
  a bottom bar and save the ☰ menu for long tails and short visits.
</Callout>
