Most framework debates argue the wrong layer: syntax taste and benchmark
deltas, when the costs that actually compound are staffing, ecosystem gaps,
and the migration you'll face in year four. This page is the **lens** — the
axes to judge any candidate against — not a filled-in matrix ranking
contenders.

<Callout variant="info" title="The lens, not a leaderboard">
  React is the only framework documented in this category so far, so it serves
  as the worked example against each axis below. More frameworks land here as
  they're documented — but the axes are the durable part; the roster is not.
</Callout>

## The axes

| Axis                | The question it answers                                           |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Team familiarity    | Who on the team ships productively in it _today_?                 |
| Ecosystem depth     | When you need a library, does a maintained one exist?             |
| Rendering-model fit | Does its CSR/SSR/streaming story match your delivery needs?       |
| Escape hatches      | When the model doesn't fit, how gracefully can you drop below it? |
| Hiring & longevity  | Can you staff it in three years? Will it still be maintained?     |
| Maintenance cost    | What do upgrades, deprecations, and churn cost per year?          |

Weight them by your situation, not equally. A two-person team rewrites the
weights a fifty-person team uses; a content site weights rendering-model fit
where an internal dashboard weights team familiarity almost exclusively.

- **Team familiarity.** The most underrated axis and usually the decisive one.
  A framework your team knows deeply beats a "better" one they'd learn on your
  dime — velocity lost to learning a new mental model is real, current, and
  paid up front, while the promised gains arrive later and discounted. Switch
  when the current framework is _failing_ at something, not when a new one is
  merely nicer.
- **Ecosystem depth.** Every gap in the ecosystem is code you write and own
  forever. Check the specific libraries _your_ product needs — the data grid,
  the charting library, the drag-and-drop — not the aggregate package count.
- **Rendering-model fit.** Frameworks differ in how mature their SSR,
  streaming, and static-generation stories are. If your product's UX depends
  on a particular delivery strategy, that constrains the framework — work
  through [Rendering & Delivery](/docs/app-screens/rendering) first and bring
  the answer here.
- **Escape hatches.** Every abstraction eventually meets a problem it wasn't
  shaped for. What matters is whether dropping below it — raw DOM, a canvas,
  an imperative widget — is a supported seam or a fight against the framework.
- **Hiring and longevity.** You're not just choosing an API; you're choosing
  the pool of people who can maintain the code after the enthusiast who picked
  it leaves. Governance matters too: who funds the framework, and what happens
  if they stop?
- **Long-term maintenance.** The purchase price is the rewrite; the cost of
  ownership is the upgrades. How disruptive were the last three major
  versions? A framework that breaks the world every two years taxes every
  team that builds on it.

## The worked example: React

Judged honestly against each axis — strengths and the bill they come with:

- **Team familiarity:** the safest assumption in the industry. Most frontend
  hires have shipped React; the mental model, for all its
  [gotchas](/docs/frameworks/react/gotchas), is the one your next teammate already
  has.
- **Ecosystem depth:** the deepest available. Whatever niche library you need
  likely exists, maintained, with its worst bugs already found by someone
  else. The flip side is decision fatigue — React ships few opinions, so every
  project re-litigates routing, data fetching, and styling.
- **Rendering-model fit:** covers the full range — CSR, SSR, static,
  streaming with Suspense, server components — though the newer server-side
  story adds real conceptual weight and usually arrives via a meta-framework
  rather than React alone.
- **Escape hatches:** solid and supported — refs to real DOM nodes, portals,
  `flushSync`, and a native path through React Native. The coarse-grained
  re-render model is the thing you occasionally work around, and the
  [patterns](/docs/frameworks/react/patterns) page is largely the discipline of
  not needing to.
- **Hiring and longevity:** the easiest staffing story in frontend, backed by
  Meta and a decade of production use. The realistic risk isn't abandonment;
  it's churn in the recommended way to do things.
- **Maintenance cost:** core upgrades have been gentler than the ecosystem
  around them — the expensive migrations tend to be the meta-framework's, not
  React's.

The honest summary: React is the low-regret default whose real costs are
paradox-of-choice and the discipline its render model demands — not any
missing capability.

## How to run the decision

<DoDont>
  <Do title="Weight the axes for your team, then score">
    Write down the two or three axes that dominate your situation, judge each
    candidate against them, and let the boring answer win. "The team knows it
    and the ecosystem covers our needs" is a complete justification.
  </Do>
  <Dont title="Choose on benchmarks or novelty">
    Rendering micro-benchmarks measure a cost most products never feel, and "the
    team is excited to learn it" is a training budget wearing an
    architecture-decision costume. Both are real inputs; neither is the
    decision.
  </Dont>
</DoDont>

One more constraint that outranks preference: if you need native mobile from
the same codebase or the same team, the framework question is partly answered
before taste enters — pick along the axis of where your product must run.
